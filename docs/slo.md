# Service level objectives

TelcoPulse defines three SLOs over a rolling 30-day window. They are written as code in [`infrastructure/slo/telcopulse.yml`](../infrastructure/slo/telcopulse.yml) (Sloth `prometheus/v1` format). `scripts/generate-slo-rules.sh` renders them with a pinned Sloth image into [`infrastructure/prometheus/slo-rules.yml`](../infrastructure/prometheus/slo-rules.yml): SLI error ratios for the 5m, 30m, 1h, 2h, 6h, 1d, 3d and 30d windows, plus objective, budget, burn-rate and remaining-budget series labelled `sloth_slo`. Do not edit the generated file; change the spec and regenerate. `scripts/generate-slo-rules.sh --check` fails when the committed output is stale. Each SLI is a raw error ratio whose denominator is filtered with `> 0`, so windows without completed events are not recorded at all instead of recording `0/0 = NaN`. A single NaN sample would make Sloth's `sum_over_time` 30-day average, and therefore the remaining budget, NaN until it left retention. Sloth's own alerts are disabled. The burn-rate alerts in `rules.yml` read the generated `slo:sli_error:ratio_rate*` series and add traffic floors and stable incident identities. What happens when a budget runs out is set by the [error budget policy](error-budget-policy.md).

| SLO | SLI (good events / valid events) | Objective | Durable view | Burn alert |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `purchase-success` | Completed purchases without a technical failure / completed purchases | 99.9% | ITOC Overview, PostgreSQL | `BusinessSuccessRateLow` |
| `purchase-latency` | Completed purchases, any outcome, with creation-to-completion ≤ 2 s / completed purchases | 99% | ITOC Overview, PostgreSQL | `PurchaseLatencyBudgetBurn` |
| `notification-freshness` | First notification receipts ≤ 30 s after the purchase outcome / first receipts | 99% | Prometheus only | `NotificationFreshnessBudgetBurn` |

All three alerts use the same multiwindow, multi-burn-rate shape. The fast branch needs both the 5m and 1h error ratios above 14.4× the budget (2% of the 30-day budget in an hour). The slow branch needs both the 30m and 6h ratios above 6× (5% in six hours). The fast branch needs at least five valid events in 5m, the slow branch at least thirty in 30m. Each alert fires at SEV-2 after 30 seconds and opens one incident per episode.

## Purchase success

The ITOC Overview displays an SRE-style objective for the selected development or staging environment. It uses durable, **completed** `transactions` in a rolling 30-day window, queried from PostgreSQL when the overview refreshes. Processing workflows are excluded until they commit an outcome. Each committed outcome is classified by `error_code` (`domain.FailureClass`). A `SUCCESS` is good. A **business decline** (currently `INSUFFICIENT_BALANCE`) is an expected rule-based refusal: it counts as good for the SLI, does not consume error budget, and is reported separately. Every other `FAILED` outcome, including deliberate fault injection and any unclassified or empty code, is a **technical failure** and counts against the objective. Idempotent replay does not add another transaction. The target is 99.9%.

For `total = success + technical + declined`, the current SLI is `100 × (total − technical) / total`. The allowed failure budget is `total × (1 − 0.999)` failure equivalents; remaining is `max(0, allowed − technical)`, and consumed is `100 × technical / allowed`. Consumption can exceed 100% after the objective is breached. The budget is traffic-based and fractional at low volume: one technical failure may consume much more than 100% when fewer than 1,000 purchases were observed. When `total = 0`, the current SLI and budget consumption are **unknown**, never 100% or healthy.

This measures the success of the synthetic customer journey, not a contractual availability SLO for a real telecom service. Business declines no longer affect the objective, so the metric is far less sensitive to the test-traffic mix; a sudden rise in the declined share is still visible in the overview and `telcopulse:business_decline_ratio:increase1h`, and may indicate a defect worth investigating. The dashboard's one-hour success trend and the Prometheus `BusinessSuccessRateLow` alert use different windows and may disagree without either being wrong. Review transaction error codes, sample size and window before interpreting a breach. The one-hour overview also returns `technical_failed` and `business_declined`; the dashboard's success rate there remains the raw business success rate.

The Prometheus alert estimates how quickly the 30-day objective's error budget is being spent: a technical failure fraction (`transaction_total{status="FAILED",failure_class="technical"}`) above 1.44% in both 5m and 1h windows is a 14.4× fast burn; above 0.6% in both 30m and 6h windows is a 6× sustained burn. It also requires at least five terminal outcomes in the 5m window for the fast branch or thirty in the 30m window for the sustained branch. Either branch fires the same SEV-2 alert after 30 seconds, so a single firing episode creates one incident. Empty/healthy traffic remains silent. New deployments may have less history than the longer range and can still fire when the shorter window has enough outcomes. These windows use process-local Prometheus counters, not the durable 30-day ledger; a gateway crash between commit and observation can omit an outcome. The alert is an investigation signal, not an external page or a proof of customer impact.

The provisioned [Grafana operations dashboard](http://localhost:3002/d/telcopulse-operations) plots both pairs of burn-rate windows in budget multiples, with threshold lines at 14.4× and 6×, a fast-burn panel for the latency and freshness SLOs, and Sloth's remaining-budget series. That remaining budget covers only the two days Prometheus retains locally, not 30 days. An alert may clear as recent traffic recovers while the durable 30-day SLO still reflects older failures. In local demo mode, `python3 tests/integration/slo_burn.py` starts a bounded 30-second `payment-decline` simulation, creates six durable technical failures (stopping the simulation in a `finally` block) and checks the recorded burn rate, firing alert and ingested incident; run it only against the local synthetic stack.

The API includes `purchase_slo` in `GET /api/v1/overview?environment=development|staging`, with exact start/end timestamps, counts (`failed` = `technical_failed` + `business_declined`), target, current percentage, allowed failures, remaining failure equivalents and consumed percentage. The query reads the persisted ledger; Prometheus's process-local counter is a separate operational signal and may miss increments around a crash. See [operational measurements](observability.md) and the [high error-rate runbook](runbooks/high-error-rate.md).

## Purchase latency

The gateway records `duration_ms` when a purchase reaches its final outcome, measured from its creation and including background recovery. It also observes the same value in `transaction_duration_seconds{environment,status}`. A purchase is **slow** when this exceeds 2,000 ms. The SLI counts every completed outcome, because a slow technical failure is still a slow customer experience. A purchase held in Processing during a dependency outage counts once it completes, and usually counts as slow. Purchases still processing are not counted yet.

The overview API returns `purchase_latency_slo` beside `purchase_slo`, from the same durable 30-day query: `total` (completed purchases with a recorded duration), `slow`, target 99%, `threshold_ms`, allowed slow purchases (`total / 100`), remaining budget and consumed percentage. As with purchase success, no traffic is unknown, never healthy. The ITOC Overview shows it as **Synthetic purchase latency objective**. The Prometheus SLI uses the histogram's `le="2"` bucket, so the two views agree except for process-local counter loss around a gateway crash.

`ServiceLatencyHigh` (per-service HTTP P95 above 1 s) remains a diagnostic cause signal. `PurchaseLatencyBudgetBurn` is the customer-journey symptom that measures budget. The [high latency runbook](runbooks/high-latency.md) applies to both.

## Notification freshness

Notification-service observes `notification_delivery_freshness_seconds{environment}` when it commits the **first** durable receipt for a purchase event: the time from the event's `occurred_at` (when the gateway committed the outcome and its outbox row) to that commit. The delay therefore includes outbox publication, Kafka transit, consumer lag and injected delays. Kafka redelivery of an already delivered event is not observed again. Operator reprocessing of dead-lettered records is excluded and tracked by the dead-letter register. Producer/consumer clock skew can make small ages negative; they count as zero.

The SLI measures only notifications that **were delivered**. An event still waiting in an outbox or behind a stopped consumer is invisible until it arrives, and it then counts as late. `EventPublicationDelayed` and `NotificationConsumerLag` cover that blind spot while it is happening. There is no PostgreSQL-backed 30-day view yet; the Prometheus series is process-local and resets on restart.

## Verification

- `promtool test rules` covers each burn alert's fast branch, a within-objective case and sparse traffic, together with the existing purchase-success cases (now evaluated on the Sloth recordings).
- `python3 tests/integration/slo_objectives.py` makes eight staging purchases slower than 2 s with a bounded database-latency simulation. It expects `PurchaseLatencyBudgetBurn`, an ingested incident, eight more slow purchases in the durable latency SLO, and the [release gate](error-budget-policy.md#release-gate) blocking STAGING and PRODUCTION_LIKE while DEV stays advisory. It then delays ten development notifications with a kafka-consumer-lag simulation and expects late deliveries and `NotificationFreshnessBudgetBurn`. Both simulations stop in `finally` blocks.
